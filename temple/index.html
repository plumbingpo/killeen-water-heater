Keeping Your Temple Water Heater Running Longer

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is simple maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, lift the T&P valve lever annually to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which limits scalding risk and cuts down on scale. Tankless owners should plan on descaling every year if their water is hard.

Age matters too. Most tank water heaters last 8 to 12 years, tankless units often reach 20 with regular descaling, and heat pump models typically run 10 to 15. If your heater is past that window and showing symptoms like discolored hot water, rumbling during heating, moisture around the base, or hot water that runs out sooner than it used to, a planned replacement is nearly always less expensive and far less stressful than an emergency one after the tank lets go.

If warning signs do show up, acting quickly keeps them small. A dripping relief valve might point to excess pressure or a missing expansion tank, both modest fixes. The same drip left alone for a season can wear out the valve and stress the tank until a seam gives way, and a small repair becomes a full replacement and a cleanup. It's smart to keep our number saved on your phone so you can reach us at the first sign of trouble. Early intervention saves time, money, and cold showers.

Typical Water Heater Issues in Temple

Around Temple, water heater trouble usually trace back to a handful of causes. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, hard water may be a real factor: dissolved minerals drop out as sediment on the tank floor, forcing the burner or lower element to work harder, which produces the rumbling and popping many homeowners hear during a heating cycle. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, which is why regular descaling matters so much where the water is hard.

Then there's age. When the protective anode rod is gone, usually somewhere in the second half of a tank's life, the steel begins corroding from within, and a slow leak at the base often shows up next. The area has a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, which means we service both sides: pilot assemblies, gas valves, and flues on gas units, and elements, thermostats, and breakers on electric ones.

Homes on private wells may bring iron, sulfur, and grit into the tank, and a sulfur smell in the hot water usually points to a reaction with the anode rod that a different rod can fix. Older installations often need code updates when a heater is replaced: an expansion tank where a check valve or pressure regulator creates a closed system, a properly routed T&P discharge line, drain pans where a leak would hit finished floors, and seismic straps in areas that require them. Every one of these problems can be fixed, and most are much cheaper to address early.
