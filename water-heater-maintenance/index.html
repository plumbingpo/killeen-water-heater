Most water heaters do not simply wear out; they rust out or scale up, and both problems can be held off for years. As water heats, dissolved minerals drop out of solution and settle into a layer of sediment on the tank floor. On a gas unit, that layer sits between the flame and the water, so the steel runs hotter and the tank starts to rumble and pop. On an electric unit, it buries the lower element until the element burns out. Meanwhile the anode rod, a metal rod designed to corrode so the tank does not, dissolves a little every month. Once it is gone, the tank itself becomes the target. In Killeen, TX, we see this pattern constantly, and most of it was preventable.

Our maintenance visit is built to interrupt both problems. We drain and flush the tank until the water runs clear, pull and inspect the anode rod and recommend a replacement when it is mostly consumed, and test the temperature and pressure relief valve to confirm it opens and reseats. On gas units we inspect the burner, pilot or igniter, thermocouple or flame sensor, flue baffle, and venting for proper draft. On electric units we check element resistance and thermostat operation and look at the wiring for heat damage. We check the expansion tank's air charge against your water pressure, confirm the thermostat setting is near 120 degrees to limit scalding, and look for early leaks at fittings and valves.

If you have a tankless unit, the routine changes but the goal is the same. Minerals coat the heat exchanger over time, reducing efficiency and eventually causing shutdowns. Our technicians connect a circulation pump to the isolation valves and run a descaling solution through the exchanger, clean the water filter, inspect the venting, and check the condensate drain on condensing models. Warning Signs to Watch For

A heater that is starting to struggle rarely hides it for long, and the clues are easy to spot once you know them. These are the red flags our technicians see most often before a breakdown:

  • Rumbling, popping, or crackling from the tank as it heats
  • A smell like rotten eggs that shows up only on the hot side
  • Hot water that looks cloudy or rust-colored
  • Moisture, drips, or rust around the base or fittings
  • Scale warnings or intermittent shutdowns on a tankless system
  • No memory of the last time the water heater was serviced
  • Energy bills creeping up with no change in hot water use
  • Less hot water available than when the tank was new

Why This Job Calls for a Pro

There is a short list of things you can do yourself: watching for leaks, keeping the area around the heater clear, and noting new noises. Beyond that, the work gets riskier. Draining a tank means handling scalding water and a drain valve that may not close again if it is old and full of sediment. Pulling an anode rod often takes serious torque on a fitting that has not moved in years. Testing a T&P valve on an older heater can leave it dripping afterward. Water Heater Care Tips From Our Technicians

A few preventive steps can help you avoid a cold shower or a wet floor down the road. Here are our recommendations:

  • Descale tankless units once a year, or more often in hard water
  • Look around the base of the heater periodically for moisture
  • Keep the thermostat near 120 degrees to limit scalding and slow sediment buildup
  • Treat hard water to cut down on sediment and scale
  • Replace a heavily consumed anode rod to extend the tank's life
  • Do not store boxes, paint, or chemicals against the water heater
  • Schedule a flush every year, or every six months if you have hard water
